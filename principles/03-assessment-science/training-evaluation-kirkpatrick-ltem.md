# Training Evaluation: Kirkpatrick Levels and LTEM

## One-line claim

Evaluate training at the level that answers the decision being made: treat learner reactions as evidence about the course experience only, measure learning as delayed decision-making and task performance, measure transfer with evidence from the job rather than from the learner, and never infer a higher level from success at a lower one.

## Evidence base

Kirkpatrick's four levels, introduced in a 1959-1960 article series and codified in Kirkpatrick and Kirkpatrick (2006), sort training outcomes into reaction (how learners felt about the training), learning (what they acquired), behavior (what they do on the job), and results (what changed for the organization). The framework is the field's common vocabulary, and practice clusters at its bottom rung: Arthur, Bennett, Edens, and Bell (2003) reported 2002 industry benchmarking data in which 78% of organizations collected reaction measures, 32% measured learning, and 7% measured results. The foundational test of whether the rungs predict one another is Alliger, Tannenbaum, Bennett, Traver, and Shotland's (1997) meta-analysis of 34 studies and 115 correlations in *Personnel Psychology*. They split reactions into affective (liking the training) and utility (judged usefulness for the job), and split learning into immediate knowledge, retention, and behavior or skill demonstration. Utility reactions related more strongly to learning and to on-the-job transfer than affective reactions did, and utility reactions were stronger correlates of transfer than immediate or retained knowledge scores were. The levels intercorrelated weakly, which Arthur et al. (2003) read as evidence that each level samples a different facet of the criterion space; they state the consequence directly: whether trainees liked a program tells evaluators little, if anything, about how much they learned.

Sitzmann, Brown, Casper, Ely, and Zimmerman's (2008) meta-analysis in the *Journal of Applied Psychology* is the best current evidence on what reactions measure. Reactions primarily captured characteristics of the course: instructional style (rho = .66) and human interaction (rho = .56) were the strongest predictors. Reactions predicted pre-to-post change in motivation (beta = .51) and self-efficacy (beta = .24) and were more sensitive to course characteristics than affective and cognitive learning outcomes were; unlike Alliger et al. (1997), Sitzmann et al. found that affective and utility reactions did not differ in their relationships with learning outcomes. Reactions are a valid index of course experience and post-training motivation, not a proxy for learning. Arthur et al.'s (2003) meta-analysis of 397 effect sizes found mean training effects of d = 0.60 on reaction criteria (k = 15), 0.63 on learning (k = 234), 0.62 on behavior (k = 122), and 0.62 on results (k = 26), and cautioned that an averaged "overall effectiveness" figure is questionable because the criteria measure distinct facets; the criteria were also collected at different delays (reactions immediately, learning at a mean of 26 days, behavior at 134 days, results at 159 days). Kraiger, Ford, and Salas (1993) replaced the single learning level with a construct-based classification: cognitive outcomes (verbal knowledge, knowledge organization, cognitive strategies), skill-based outcomes (compilation and automaticity), and affective outcomes (attitudinal and motivational), each paired with measures that fit the construct. Bell, Tannenbaum, Ford, Noe, and Kraiger's (2017) century review of training research in the same journal traces training-criteria work toward these multidimensional outcomes and toward the role of the trainee and the training context.

The principal boundary condition is that the levels are a taxonomy, not a causal chain. Holton (1996) argued in *Human Resource Development Quarterly* that the four levels classify outcomes but do not specify causal links or the intervening variables that govern them (motivation to learn, trainability, job attitudes, transfer conditions), and he specified a new role for participant reactions instead of treating them as the first outcome rung. Arthur et al. (2003) add that the learning-to-behavior link is hard to show empirically because on-the-job behavior depends on whether the work environment offers the opportunity and support to use the skill. Will Thalheimer's Learning-Transfer Evaluation Model (LTEM, 2018, version 12; a practitioner framework, not peer-reviewed) refines the ladder into eight tiers: attendance; activity (attention, interest, participation); learner perceptions, split into questions that probe learning effectiveness and questions that probe satisfaction or reputation; knowledge recitation and knowledge retention; decision-making competence; task competence; transfer (assisted and full); and effects of transfer. Tiers 5 and 6 each have an immediate form and a remembered form measured after several days, and LTEM labels attendance, activity, satisfaction-type perceptions, and usually knowledge as inadequate to validate learning; the 2024 version 13 relabels tiers 6 and 7 as task performance and transfer to work performance. The tier order is a design argument, not an empirically validated hierarchy. Evidence strength is moderate: the negative claim (reactions do not stand in for learning or transfer) rests on several meta-analyses, while the positive prescription (which ladder to use and where to stop) is framework-level.

## When to apply

- **Evaluation plan requested** - Write the evaluation plan with the objectives, not after launch;
  each measure names the decision it informs and the person who will act on it.
- **Learner survey about to ship** - Audit each item for what it can support. Satisfaction items
  describe the experience; they cannot certify learning (Sitzmann et al., 2008).
- **Module completed** - Measure decision-making and task competence on realistic scenarios, then
  repeat after several days; a recall quiz alone is knowledge recitation, which LTEM flags as
  usually inadequate.
- **On-job follow-up window open** - Once learners have had real opportunities to perform the skill
  (weeks to months; behavior criteria averaged 134 days post-training in Arthur et al., 2003), collect
  transfer evidence from supervisors and work records.
- **Effectiveness evidence requested** - Answer at the tier the sponsor's decision needs (funding,
  renewal, certification); report lower tiers separately as diagnostics, not substitutes.
- **Course revision decision pending** - Before revising, retiring, or scaling a course, locate the
  tier where performance breaks down; the fix differs for a learning failure and a transfer failure.

## When NOT to apply

- **Target job performance is undefined or contested** - If no one can name the on-job behavior or
  result, higher-tier measures have nothing to target. Run a performance analysis first (see
  [action-mapping-performance-analysis](../02-instructional-design/action-mapping-performance-analysis.md)).
- **Decision and task instruments lack validity evidence** - A tier 5 or 6 label on an unvalidated
  scenario bank or an unreliable rater checklist overstates the evidence. Establish score meaning
  first (see [validity-reliability](../03-assessment-science/validity-reliability.md)).
- **Single tutoring turn or one-off lookup** - One question or one job-aid lookup warrants an
  embedded check of the answer, not a multi-tier evaluation study.
- **Outcomes are emergent by design** - When useful outcomes are not known in advance, a fixed
  ladder misses what changed. Collect open-ended evidence of change first, then write criteria.
- **Learners have no transfer opportunity yet** - Pre-employment students cannot show tier 7
  transfer inside the course window. Stop at remembered task competence and schedule an employer
  follow-up; do not fill the gap with learners' self-predictions of future use.

## How to apply

- **Start from the decision, then pick the tier** - Write the question ("release this module?",
  "renew this contract?"), the decision-maker, and the action each result triggers. Measure at the
  tier that answers it; collect lower tiers only as diagnostics.
- **Classify each objective by outcome type** - Tag each terminal objective cognitive, skill-based,
  or affective (Kraiger et al., 1993) and match the measure: scenario decisions for cognitive, observed
  performance with a checklist, time, and errors for skill-based, self-efficacy scales for affective.
- **Build learning measures at LTEM tiers 5 and 6** - Write decision items that put the learner in a
  realistic situation and require a choice; build task measures that require the full performance.
  Administer once at module end and again after several days with parallel content.
- **Rewrite the learner survey, then fence it off** - Replace liking items with items about
  usefulness, opportunity to apply, and confidence on named tasks. Report the survey as course
  experience and motivation (Sitzmann et al., 2008), in its own section, never as a learning score.
- **Plan transfer measurement with the workplace** - Agree with supervisors on a job observation
  checklist and work-record indicators (rework, error reports, audit findings), and record opportunity
  to perform and supervisor support, because transfer depends on the environment (Holton, 1996).
- **Diagnose across tiers, not up a chain** - Strong task competence with weak transfer implicates the
  work environment; weak task competence with strong reactions implicates a pleasant, uninstructive design.
- **Keep three jobs separate** - This chapter decides which evidence to collect. Compute gains with
  [pre-post-assessment-effect-size](../03-assessment-science/pre-post-assessment-effect-size.md),
  check score meaning with validity-reliability, and report each tier with its timing and n; never
  average tiers into one effectiveness number (Arthur et al., 2003).

## Common misapplications

- **Reporting satisfaction as learning** - A 4.6 out of 5 average describes the experience. Reactions
  track instructional style and post-training motivation more than learning (Sitzmann et al., 2008).
- **Treating the levels as a causal chain** - "They liked it, so they learned it; they learned it, so
  they use it" is the assumption Alliger et al. (1997) and Holton (1996) undercut.
- **Calling a recall quiz "Level 2"** - An immediate fact quiz is LTEM tier 4 recitation and says
  little about task competence, though Kirkpatrick's learning level admits it.
- **Using self-report as behavior evidence** - "I have applied this on the job" is a learner
  perception. Transfer evidence comes from observation, work products, or records.
- **Measuring only at the end of the session** - Without a remembered (delayed) measure, a forgotten
  skill looks the same as a retained one.
- **Chasing results without the middle tiers** - A results metric that moves cannot be attributed to
  training, and one that does not move cannot be diagnosed, unless learning and transfer were measured.

## Examples across domains

**Avionics (CAET lesson): evaluation plan for the altimeter and transponder test module.**

*Setup.* A CAET module teaches the tests required by 14 CFR 91.411 and 91.413: static system,
altimeter, and altitude-reporting tests under Part 43 Appendix E, and ATC transponder tests under
Appendix F, every 24 calendar months and after defined maintenance. The prior version reported a
course-satisfaction average of 4.6 out of 5 as its effectiveness evidence. The designer rejects it as
evidence of learning: reactions track course style and motivation (Sitzmann et al., 2008), and a
satisfied trainee can still miss a 91.411(a)(2) static test after a port replacement.

*Application.* The plan names the decision (release the module and certify trainees for supervised
shop work) and builds evidence by tier. Tier 3: a short survey asks which Appendix F test the trainee
feels least ready to run, reported separately as course experience. Tier 4 is limited to prerequisites
(the 24-calendar-month interval). Tier 5 uses scenario decision items: "The static system was opened to
replace a static port; the transponder check is current. What test is required before IFR return to
service?" (Appendix E(a) per 91.411(a)(2)); "Mode C reports 140 feet above the altimeter at one test
point: pass or fail?" (fail; Appendix E(c) allows 125 feet); "The altimeter was tested to 18,000 feet;
can the aircraft fly IFR at FL250?" (no, per 91.411(d)). Tier 6 is a sim practical with a virtual
transponder test set: the trainee runs the Appendix F sequence (ATCRBS reply frequency 1090 ±3 MHz,
receiver MTL -73 ±4 dBm, no more than 1% replies with P2 equal to P1, Mode S address check with the assigned
and at least two incorrect addresses, squitter about once per second), makes pass or fail calls, and
writes the record with the date and maximum altitude tested per Appendix E(d). A checklist scores
sequence, values, calls, and record; the practical repeats after seven days with a different fault.

*Follow-up.* At 60-90 days, the employer repair station, which holds the ratings 91.411(b) and
91.413(c) require, supplies tier 7 evidence: supervisor sign-off on the trainee's first supervised
91.411 and 91.413 jobs, plus work-order data on rework and comebacks for altitude-reporting squawks.
Tier 8 compares the shop's comeback rate for these jobs across cohorts, read with caution. If
practical scores are high but sign-offs lag, the designer checks test-set access and supervision before
touching the lessons; if the "which test after which maintenance" items fail, that segment is revised.

**Corporate leadership development: renewal decision for a new-manager feedback program.**

*Setup.* A company runs a three-day program for new first-line managers on giving performance
feedback and running one-on-one meetings. HR's report cites 4.7 out of 5 satisfaction and "95% would
recommend." The finance lead must decide whether to renew the vendor contract and asks whether the
program worked. The designer notes that neither figure answers that question.

*Application.* The plan names the decision (renew, redesign, or drop) and builds evidence by tier.
Tier 3 keeps three usefulness items and reports them as experience. Self-efficacy for difficult
conversations, measured before and after, is reported as an affective outcome (Kraiger et al., 1993)
that reactions do track (Sitzmann et al., 2008), not as proof of skill. Tier 5 uses
video situational-judgment items: the manager watches a one-on-one in which an employee deflects and
picks the next move. Tier 6 is a role-play with a trained actor, scored on a behaviorally anchored
checklist (describes the specific behavior and its impact, asks for the employee's view, agrees on a
dated next step), run at the program's end and again three weeks later.

*Follow-up.* At three and six months, tier 7 evidence comes from direct reports' pulse-survey ratings
of feedback quality and from one-on-one completion records, compared with managers waiting for the
next cohort. Tier 8 (team engagement, regretted attrition) is tracked but not attributed to the
program alone. Role-play scores rise while direct-report ratings stay flat; the follow-up finds that
the managers' own leaders skip one-on-ones. The fix is a leadership expectation with an owner, not a
course rewrite, and the renewal decision rests on tier 6 and 7 evidence instead of satisfaction.

## Quality signal

The evaluation is working when every reported metric carries its tier and the decision it serves, each terminal objective has at least one decision-making or task measure repeated after several days, and transfer evidence comes from a source other than the learner (observation or work records). Track course satisfaction and delayed task scores side by side across cohorts; a revision, renewal, or certification decision that cites only satisfaction, or a report that merges tiers into one number, is a defect.
